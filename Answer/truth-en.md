## I. The Assessment

The designation SPARTAN-II had been in internal use at Section III since 2511, when Catherine Halsey's proposal - Generation II of the old ORION project - was formally launched and renamed. It was built around colonial children from the start: genetically and cognitively screened candidates, not a program that began with adult volunteer trials and pivoted to children later. Candidate screening ran continuously from 2511 onward, cross-referenced against the outer colonies' vaccination program genetic records, and slowly built a pool of 150 candidates.

In 2516, ONI Section III completed a revised risk assessment on colonial insurgency - a revision, because there had been an earlier one. The conclusion wasn't reassuring: centrifugal pressure in the outer colonies was already outpacing UNSC's normal capacity to respond, and without intervention, the math pointed toward large-scale civil war within years - and that was before the Covenant had entered anyone's threat model at all.

That assessment was an internal re-confirmation of a program already running, not the moment children entered the picture for the first time. Catherine Halsey read it three times. Her reply to Section III didn't say *I agree*. It said: "You asked me whether I could live with it. I don't think that is the right question. The right question is whether the colonies survive long enough to ask me anything at all. I have run the projections three more times since we spoke. They do not improve." She asked for approval to move from the 150-candidate screening pool to actual acquisition.

## II. Acquisition

The 2517 acquisition directive didn't say *to fight the Covenant*, because the Covenant wasn't on anyone's list of things to worry about yet. It said colonial insurgency risk. Seventy-five children were selected out of the 150-candidate pool. They were taken without consent - not their parents', and not their own.

The hard part was never taking them. The hard part was making an entire colonial administration, and the families, and the neighbors, believe it had ended reasonably. The answer was the flash-clone substitution protocol: every selected child was replaced with a full-body flash clone - an accelerated-growth construct with a known, inherent limit on viability, not a purpose-built defect. Left to run its course, it would fail within a short window in a way that would read as an ordinary natural or medical death to any attending physician. That existing limitation was the point: the local doctor closing each case would have no reason to suspect anything, and no need to be read into the program at all. The clone would die of "natural causes," a death certificate would be issued through the normal process and filed through the normal process, and nobody would have a reason to ask for an autopsy.

Eli Okafor (Eridanus II), Talia Wren (Madrigal), Dominic Farrow (Skopje), Samuel Voight (Eridanus II), and seventy-one other children among the seventy-five selected died of "natural causes" in colonial records this way. Each local death certificate was signed by an ordinary colonial physician who had no idea any of this existed; they were simply closing what looked, to them, like a completely normal case. Dr. Miriam Castel's role at ONI Section III was program-side medical oversight - reviewing case files after the fact to confirm each closure held up, never present for any of them herself. She later added a note in the margin of one file, in a different hand, clearly written after the fact: "I reviewed and signed off on three of these case files before I understood what I was actually confirming. I am not proud of that, and I am not going to pretend I didn't have a choice." The fourth, Voight's, was reviewed and signed off by Dr. R. Achebe - not because of any special arrangement, just because the medical staff involved in this were never only Castel.

## III. Training

The children were sent to Reach, into the training program Chief Mendez ran. CPO M. Kade was one of Mendez's instructors, assigned a small group off the SPINDLE list rather than the whole cohort - and later one of the few to leave anything in writing that admitted the weight of it. In a memo written years afterward, explicitly marked for the sealed file and not a report anyone would review, he wrote: "My own assignment was a small group, not the whole cohort - that was Mendez's program from the first candidate to the last. But the ones I had, I trained personally, start to finish. I watched some of them not survive augmentation. I watched the rest of them become something this species needed a great deal more than it ever admitted to needing. I don't know if that makes it right. I know I'd do it again, and I know that scares me more than anything the Covenant has thrown at us."

At the end of the same memo, he added one more paragraph - by his own account, because the file was sealed anyway and nobody would really read it: "One more thing. I still think about 07-B. Skopje kid, angriest six-year-old I ever met, best reflexes in the whole cohort by the second year. The official log on that one reads as a medical discharge. That is not what I watched happen in the augmentation bay, and I was standing right there. I did not write it up that way. I don't know who did, or why."

## IV. Augmentation

In 2525, the candidates underwent augmentation at roughly fourteen. The outcomes were not uniform:

Eli Okafor did not survive the procedure.

Talia Wren and Samuel Voight succeeded and entered active service, designated Spartan-108 and Spartan-128 respectively.

Dominic Farrow's outcome - **deliberately unresolved, not an unfinished thread** - has three incomplete, mutually contradictory sources. The official casualty log reads "augmentation failure, permanent disability, discharged." Kade's memo insists he watched Farrow die in the augmentation bay. A separate 2525 cryogenic recovery transfer authorization records him as judged "clinically non-viable" at the time and placed into long-term suspension, with no follow-up ever filed. None of the three documents is complete, and none is fully overruled by the other two. Nobody - including Petrov, who would later read all three - ever sets out to decide which one is true.

## V. The War, and After

SPARTAN-II's existence had been public knowledge since well before the war ended - UNSC had been leaning on their combat record for morale and recruiting purposes since at least 2547. By the time the main phase of the Human-Covenant War ended in 2552, that reputation - the armor, the record, the near-mythic image, known across colonies and Earth alike - was simply more widespread than ever, not something that had just appeared. UNSC entered postwar reconstruction from 2553. The one thing that stayed unpublished, then and now, was the origin. By 2555, when this story takes place, it still hadn't been confirmed through any public channel.

## VI. A Body Nobody Finished Cleaning Up

In 2547, the legacy system SPINDLE was decommissioned. Its records split by classification: standard cases moved to LEDGER; classified and historical material was supposed to go to a permanent archive, but in practice only made it as far as CAIRN - a node that was only ever meant to be a short-lived staging mirror. The decommission schedule was never actually completed, and CAIRN was left where it sat, its security posture looking like a storage closet nobody had cleared out in forty years, not an active top-secret data center.

Cmdr. I. Petrov, on behalf of ONI Section III, signed Disposition Order 2547-014: the entire batch of acquisition-era material would be "retained, restricted, not destroyed." That was his official position - written down, signed, procedurally defensible.

The same day, something else happened. It left no formal paperwork at all.

SPINDLE's standard decommission procedure required every item in indefinite custodial storage to be checked against an end-of-system checklist: continuing custody, or final disposition. The checklist didn't care what the holding was. It only asked whether anyone was still answering for its existence. Subject 07-B - Dominic Farrow's cryogenic suspension unit - came up on that list like everything else.

A proper continuing-custody review for 07-B would have required pulling his augmentation record, his training record, and his original case closure into the same room, in front of people cleared for exactly one of those three files each. Petrov could not identify a reviewing body positioned to hold all three at once without the acquisition program surfacing to people who weren't supposed to know it had ever happened. In 2547, with the war still ongoing, that exposure risk wasn't something he was prepared to let a subordinate carry.

So he didn't send it up for review. He recoded the holding's custodial status himself, outside the checklist, before it ever reached anyone else's desk. It now read as continuing, no action pending. The entry carried no authorization, because he had none to give it. CAIRN's access log happened to catch a purely technical trace of it: `i.petrov CUSTODY_STATUS_SET 07-B`, 2547-02-11 - not a formal decision record, just a system logging who touched which field and when, with no explanation and no one asking for one.

Petrov later put the whole thing into a private account only the highest clearance would ever see. He was direct about what he didn't know: he did not know whether 07-B was alive or dead. He had read the three contradictory records more than once, and he was not going to write a fourth version here. What he had been able to do was make sure no automated process would ever raise the question for him on a schedule he hadn't chosen. "The transfer everyone keeps asking about never happened. The 2547 migration already moved this holding as far as it was ever going to go - onto this node, with the rest of the acquisition-era material, pending onward transfer to permanent archival custody. That onward transfer is what never happened, and never will now, because a case that generates no review activity also generates no reason to finish moving it. Concealment, in this instance, took the form of nothing happening, on purpose, for thirty years."

## VII. Someone Happened to See It

The same day, in the same migration batch, a temporary records clerk - N. Okafor - was reindexing Eridanus II and Madrigal cases, routine work. She saw Eli Okafor's old case get reindexed, carrying a transfer reference that shouldn't have existed.

She was Naomi Okafor. Eli's mother.

She had never really believed her son died of illness. But before that moment, she had nothing specific to point to. She had no access to the restricted system - only her own standing in Records, which she used, quietly, for almost eight years, cross-searching for the same pattern. She found Wren. She found Farrow. She never found Voight - her reach was genuinely limited, not an oversight, just the real edge of what she could touch from where she sat.

What actually drove her to open a case wasn't a mother's instinct. It was something specific she could point to: a surviving fragment of a 2547 migration index showing that Farrow's case had been reprocessed decades after it closed. Not migration noise. Something someone had actually touched.

By 2555, she decided she'd gathered enough. She reached out to an independent hacker through a handle - without revealing who she was, or that Eli had been her son. She gave only three names, because that was all she had confirmed herself. She wasn't asking whether they were really dead. She was asking who authorized that transfer, where the original records went, and - why a death record closed thirty years ago had been touched again in 2547.

"I don't need a theory. I need the record that made them change his file."

## VIII. The Answer

Here it is: a routine check during SPINDLE's decommission put 07-B's cryogenic suspension unit on a review list. An officer, before that review could formally trigger, quietly recoded its custodial status to "no action needed," without authorization, because a formal review would have forced three records he believed should never be cross-read into the same room at once. He did it not because he knew whether Farrow was alive or dead, but because he didn't want anyone else to be forced to find out more than that.

Nobody destroyed anything. Nobody issued an order to cover anything up. Thirty years of silence came down to one person's unauthorized decision, and his choice afterward to let that decision stay quiet - until, one day, a leftover technical log was read by someone he never imagined would exist.

The story does not answer what actually happened to Farrow. The three contradictory records - the official casualty log, Kade's eyewitness account, the cryogenic transfer authorization - are never reconciled. That isn't a narrative gap. It's a deliberate piece of the world itself: some files simply never resolve into one version.

## IX. The Theme

If the heroes who saved humanity were built on something everyone would agree should never have happened, should that history stay buried?

Nobody in this story ever says that sentence out loud. The doctor talks about medical viability. The records clerk talks about case closure. The officer talks about operational necessity. The IT staff only ever see a broken reference. The training instructor only remembers one kid. Each of them only ever touched their own small piece of it - and that is exactly how it stayed quiet for thirty years. Not because anyone ordered a cover-up. Because no one, until now, ever saw all of it at once.

The one who finally does is whoever opens this sealed record last.
